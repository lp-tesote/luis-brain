01 · The despair

Put it all in Anthropic and Nvidia, and go home.

That's the mid-2026 investor's version of AI psychosis: a despair that nothing is investable. And the despair isn't stupid. Its logic runs clean:

The model keeps getting better at everything.
smarter every sub-version, self-improvement on the horizon
So every company built on top of one is a thin wrapper, waiting to be absorbed.
whatever you built, the next release does natively
The only value that survives is compute and the frontier weights.
the conclusion — and where the logic goes wrong

04 · The map

Ask two things of any work.

Is its correctness private and expensive to establish — the kind of truth that exists only inside someone's data? And is it walled off, locked inside a system you can't get into? Set those against how saturated the task is, and you get a map:

06 · The absorption frontier

The water keeps rising.

We keep learning to measure more of the work, and the measurable gets eaten. The retrieval, the routing, the tool use, even the reasoning policy — the apparatus that used to wrap a model is being pulled into the weights, until the wrapper is the model.

the absorption frontier · rising
a partner's trust, built over years of matters

07 · Why intelligence isn't the bottleneck

The door has a lock and a deadbolt.

A better model does not make private ground truth public. It does not hold the license, sign off on the liability, or own the firm's files — and it cannot be the party that gets sued. You can imagine a model far smarter than any person, and it still has to be let in the door.

The lock
The environment
You only get to verify whether AI did something useful inside a system once you're trusted inside it — after the security review, the integration, the contract with your name on the outcome.
The deadbolt
The user
A majority of American doctors now open OpenEvidence every day. No amount of compute buys that. Trust is built slowly, on relationships, with the user's acquiescence — not gradient descent.
08 · The job

Unglamorous translation.

An application earns its place in the untrainable corner by arranging a company's private reality so a model can act on it, handing it the tools to act, and working with the customer to change the reality of its workforce. The translation never ends — integration and maintenance run as long as the relationship does.

At a top white-shoe law firm, the M&A practice alone runs close to a thousand deals a year. The signal that matters lives at the level of the deal — and a deal has a shape: NDA, term sheet, diligence, purchase agreement, ancillaries, closing checklist. The problem the firm is actually solving sits a level above all of it: running every practice area in parallel. You can't write an eval for transforming a firm.

Real automation isn't only the model getting better. It's the product, the model, the workflow, and the firm moving together — and three of those four move at the speed of an organization. Every company has every engineer on frontier coding models; not one has changed its eng org at anything close to that speed. Adoption took a quarter. The rebuild is taking years.

09 · Selling the illegible

When you can't prove it, price it.

A company can't tell from the outside whether AI will transform its operations any better than the benchmark can. So the strongest businesses stop trying to prove it externally, get in, and price the outcome instead.

10 · The supplier objection

It only works if the model layer is a single-player game.

Why won't the lab run its own product below cost and bleed you out? That's the real version of the despair — and the model layer clearly isn't single-player. It's a three-and-a-half-way death match, with international players six months behind and a development league five times last year's size. Customers want competition among their suppliers, and labs want market share more than they want any one application dead.

Watch the market where labs compete head to head: in consumer chat, the best model has never simply won. ChatGPT held its lead through years of real competition; the share it's losing now goes to Gemini on the strength of Android and Search, not a better model. The lab rated as having the best model is barely a factor in consumer chat — it built its business in enterprise and coding instead.

If a better model can't take a rival's users in the most central application there is, it isn't going to integrate its way through a hospital's records or a bank's liability.

11 · The endgame

Someone has to decide what a good answer even is.

If the work can't be scored from outside, someone on the inside decides — and that decision is the whole game. Enough of those decisions, written down, become the benchmark. Harvey publishes one for law. Sierra publishes one for voice agents. OpenEvidence is settling what a safe clinical answer looks like.

None of this is really measurement. It's judgment about what is true and what is good, written down until it becomes the standard everyone else is measured against. A foundation lab can't author it, however smart it gets — that standing only exists inside the field. The authority lands where it already sat: the senior lawyer writes the legal benchmark, the physician defines the safe clinical answer, and "resolved" means whatever the company that already owns the customer says it does.

3×/yr
Offense is harder than defense. Choosing what to build is what the author spends the year looking for — and finds maybe three times. The model will do whatever you point it at and can't tell you what's worth pointing it at. Intent may be a scarcer input than compute.
12 · What survives

The despair is half right.

The thin wrapper layer really is being absorbed — and a lot of what looks like a company today is a thin wrapper. It's wrong about what that leaves. The direction never reverses: intelligence keeps getting cheaper, and value keeps sliding toward the few places a model can't reach. The untrainable is value with history.
