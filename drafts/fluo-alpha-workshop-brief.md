## 2. The situation, in one paragraph

The program is working and Alpha is tripling to about 100 students. The problem is that our current workshop sits on the 7th floor of an office tower that was never designed for making things. The ventilation is inadequate for real fabrication, we have been managing it with masks and limited use, and that is not an honest or sustainable setup for a hundred kids. So the question is no longer "how do we patch floor 7." It is "where should the maker program actually live next year," and that is a facilities decision only Trilogy and the building can make. Arthur is the unlock.

## 3. The building: 180 Maiden Lane (the Continental Center)

Key facts that shape everything:

- A 41-story Class A office tower from the early 1980s. We are on the **7th floor.**
- Sealed, insulated, Low-E **glass curtain wall.** The windows do not open.
- Central plant HVAC, shared and recirculating across tenants. LEED Gold and Energy Star certified.
- Office floors are rated for roughly **50 pounds per square foot;** mechanical and below-grade space is far heavier (around 150 psf or, on a cellar slab, effectively unlimited).
- It was built for white-collar tenants, not fabrication. Heavy stock comes up by elevator. It is loud and dusty when we work, which office neighbors will eventually mind.

The bottom line: this building resists a workshop everywhere except the parts nobody photographs, which is the cellar and ground floor.

## 4. The core constraint: why "they are billionaires, just cut a hole" does not work

The instinct is that money solves it and the building could just put a vent through the wall. It cannot, and this is the single most important thing to understand before any conversation:

- It is **not our building.** Alpha is a tenant. The glass facade is the landlord's building envelope.
- Cutting the curtain wall is an envelope modification, not a tenant fit-out. It compromises weatherproofing, thermal performance, the LEED and Energy Star ratings, the warranty, and the uniform look of the tower.
- It would trigger landlord refusal, DOB review, and a facade engineer. Landlords essentially never allow this for a tenant.

So the facade is off the table. The only real directions for air are: **recirculate and filter inside the room,** or **exhaust up through a shaft to a fan on the roof.** Not out the side.

A note on the roof route, since it comes up: a duct runs from our floor up an existing vertical shaft (elevator, plumbing, mechanical, or a spare chase) to a fan on the roof. We would not need any floor above 7, and we would not lease the top floor. The fan lives on the roof, which is building infrastructure. The whole thing hinges on whether a usable shaft with spare capacity runs from 7 to the roof, which only the building engineer knows. From the 7th floor it is roughly a 450-foot vertical run, which is real but routine engineering if the shaft exists.

## 6. The strategy (read this section twice)

### The Ovitz vibe, in one line

Walk in as the partner who is already thinking about their growth, never as the vendor asking for a favor. We are not requesting a better room. We are handing them the key to scaling the thing parents already love, and we have done the homework so we are the most prepared person in the meeting. Calm, generous, certain. The posture is "let me make this easy for you," not "please help me."

### Reframe from need to strength

The weak framing is "we do not have a space that works, please help us." The true framing is the opposite: we run the most successful workshop at Alpha, the school is tripling, and the only thing standing between Alpha and a signature program parents brag about is a facilities decision only Trilogy can make. We are not asking for a favor. We are handing Arthur the unlock to something he already wants.

### The Ovitz playbook (how we run this)

1. **Know more than the person across the table.** We walk in already holding the building's constraints, the real options, and rough costs. That alone decides who leads the conversation.
2. **Package, do not itemize.** We are not selling a ventilation system. We are selling next year's maker program: a hundred kids, a signature Alpha experience, the facility as the thing that makes it possible. If they are evaluating an HVAC line item, we have lost the frame. If they are evaluating whether to greenlight the crown jewel of the campus, the cost is a rounding error.
3. **Let being wanted do the work.** We quietly control the thing parents love most. We never say that crudely. We lead with proof: what students built, what parents said, what it did for tours and word of mouth.
4. **Give first, and make them the hero.** We do the legwork and frame it so the win is theirs to carry up to Trilogy leadership.
5. **The meeting has one job.** Its job is not a yes on a buildout. Its job is to get the room co-owning the vision and to extract the building information and the decision process. Keep the actual ask small and concrete.

### Honesty as leverage

We surface the air-quality reality openly. A serious operator does not scale a program to a hundred children in a space he is not proud of and hope nobody notices. Raising the standard before anyone forces us to is exactly what earns us more responsibility. "I will not put a hundred kids in a space we are not proud of" is a line of strength, not weakness.

### The lease-holder fork (a strategic question to keep live)

- If **Alpha leases** and we operate, we carry no real estate risk but stay captive to one client.
- If **Fluō holds its own space,** we take on lease liability but gain independence and turn a cost center into a profit center (Alpha as anchor tenant, plus Atelier, weekend classes, parent events, multi-school fabrication). This is the company-defining version. If we ever carry our own lease, it should buy a real industrial box in Brooklyn that doubles as a production hub, not an overpriced FiDi box.

### Where the options land (our current ranking)

1. **Phase 1, now:** Filtration on the current floor. Cheap, fast, makes the fall safe and honest. Non-negotiable, because nothing else is ready by September.
2. **Phase 2, this school year:** The on-campus flagship. **Cellar or ground floor at 180 Maiden,** in that order of likelihood. Cellar wins on availability, price, and solving load, noise, and venting at once. Ground floor wins on everything plus street visibility, but is hardest to pry loose. A walkable nearby space is the fallback only if the building gives us nothing. The Seaport is the ambitious flagship alternative.
3. **Phase 3, parallel and optional:** A cheap Brooklyn production hub that Fluō owns and operates as its own asset. Where the desks, boats, and surfboards get batch-produced and where students come a few times a year for the big builds.

The synthesis: yes, there is a world where Alpha gives us a real makerspace, but the version that works has the kids going **downstairs, not across the river.** We ask the building for cellar or ground floor first. Walkable and Seaport are the fallback and the flagship play.
